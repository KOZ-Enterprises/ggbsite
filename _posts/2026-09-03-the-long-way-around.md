I failed a programming course in high school. Not "didn't love it" failed. Failed failed. I
decided programming was not for me, went to college for mechanical engineering instead, and
spent the next decade being right about that. I was wrong.

Somewhere in there I got a master's in mechanical engineering, learned to certify pipe
fittings for nuclear submarines, and spent seven years designing and testing flight
hardware. And somewhere in there, around year eight of my career, I started writing Python
at work because the MATLAB scripts were no longer enough. I automated parts of my own job,
built little internal tools, and discovered I liked it the way I liked building my first PC
at thirteen: the kind of like that does not go away when you ignore it.

<!--more-->

## The second attempt

In 2023 I enrolled at CSU Monterey Bay for a BS in Computer Science, nights and weekends,
while working full-time as an engineer at Boeing. The failed high school course stayed with
me in a specific way: I kept waiting for the moment it would all make sense why I had
failed it, like the universe owed me an explanation. The explanation, it turns out, is
boring. It was not aptitude. It was timing, and a teenager's attention span, and a course
taught in a style that did not reach me. None of that is a personality trait.

What did help was showing up as a working engineer instead of a teenager. I already knew
how to debug, because I had spent years debugging hardware. I already knew how to break a
big problem into pieces, because that is what mechanical design is. The degree filled in
the theory and the discipline I had skipped, and it did it one night class at a time.

I finished in April 2026. The piece of paper says computer science. What it really
certifies is that I can keep a promise to myself across three years of weeknights.

## What changed

At Boeing I investigate root-cause problems on out-of-production commercial fleets: an
airplane does something it should not, and my job is figuring out what is actually going on
underneath it. The CS degree changed how I do that in three ways.

First, vocabulary. I can talk to data teams and software organizations as a peer instead of
a customer. Second, tooling. I build analysis pipelines and small internal tools that keep
an investigation honest, which beats a wall of spreadsheet tabs. Third, and most fun: AI
coding agents. I have gotten genuinely good at keeping them on task, scoping them tightly,
and catching them when they wander off and do something confidently wrong. Pointing that
capability at real engineering data problems is the most productive thing I have learned
to do with a computer since the cybercafe days.

## The fun part

For fun I train simulated drone swarms to figure out formation flying on their own, with no
central planner micromanaging them. NVIDIA Isaac Lab, shared-policy PPO, and way more math
than any hobby has any business requiring. It is the combination the whole arc was building
toward: mechanical intuition about how things move, and software that lets a swarm learn to
move that way together. Real hardware is next, assuming I do not break anything expensive.

## What did not change

The degree did not turn me into a different kind of person. I still think like a mechanical
engineer first, which means I trust empirical results over assertions, and I still have
zero tolerance for BS once there is a real technical problem in front of me. The computer
science training just gave that instinct better tools.

And yes, before you ask: I am not enrolling in anything else. The arc can stop climbing for
a minute. I have three degrees, a day job investigating airplanes, a rover, and a drone
swarm that is about to meet real hardware. That is plenty of problems to keep me busy.

The failed high school course is now a funny story I tell at the start of my bio. It took
me two decades to get here, but I got here the long way around, and it turns out the long
way is where all the good stuff happened.
