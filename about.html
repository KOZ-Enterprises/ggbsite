Gary Kuepper // Long Beach, CA

About Me

I’m an Environmental Control Systems (ECS) Analysis Engineer at Boeing and a recent Computer Science graduate from CSUMB. My route here wasn’t a straight line. It ran through a BS and MS in Mechanical Engineering, a QC role certifying pipe fittings for nuclear submarines, seven years designing and testing flight hardware at Marvin Engineering, and now Boeing, where I investigate in-service ECS problems so the cabin stays comfortable, and breathable, at 35,000 feet.

How I Got Here

The software half came later, and by choice. I failed a programming course in high school, decided it wasn’t for me, and spent a decade as a mechanical engineer before teaching myself Python on the job and realizing I’d been wrong. I went back for a BS in Computer Science at CSUMB, nights and weekends while working full-time, and graduated in April 2026. These days I live where hardware meets software: mechanical systems running on intelligent code, heading toward autonomous drone swarms and distributed robotics.

My Journey

Two mechanical degrees gave me the physical side, how machines actually move, fail, and get tested. The CS degree added the other half: the algorithms, data, and code that make them coordinate. That combination is the point. I can design the hardware, instrument the test, and write the software that runs it, which is exactly what building a drone swarm from scratch demands.

What I’m Building

I’m a firm believer in learning by building, breaking, and rebuilding. At Boeing, my day job is Environmental Control Systems (ECS) Analysis Engineer: resolving in-service safety problems to keep airplanes flying and everyone breathing onboard. Outside that, ggRover is my fun build, a semi-autonomous rover with an STM32 brain and a PS4 controller behind the wheel. GG Swarm started as my capstone project and outlived the semester: a GNN-based drone swarm design maturing from simulation toward hardware.

Beyond the Code

When I’m not at a terminal or a workbench, I’m usually out finding new experiences. I travel alone on purpose, the Anthony Bourdain way: street-level, food-first, an itinerary I build as I go, and a standing rule that history is better confronted than skipped. The shortlist never stays short for long, with Mexico, Southeast Asia, and Eastern Europe all making their case. Back home in downtown Long Beach the hobbies run in parallel: Tottenham in a Son #7 shirt, K-pop shows, and a Steam Deck thousands of hours deep in Europa Universalis IV. I read physical books before their adaptations, so the source material always gets to spoil the screen version first.

What It Adds Up To

Most of the work I have done comes down to the same thing: closing the gap between what a model predicts and what actually happens. A simulation is confident, a test result is true, and the distance between them is where I’ve spent my career. It’s also, roughly, the shape of my life.

That wasn’t a plan. A failed programming class told me programming wasn’t for me, and I believed it for about ten years, which is easy when you’re already good at something else. Then I took a quality control job I’ll describe politely, certifying pipe fittings for submarine programs, and that was the floor of my own chart. Narrow work, a low ceiling, and enough competence in it to stay forever. Being good at something you don’t want a life of is its own kind of problem.

I stopped waiting for permission somewhere in there. The ambition wasn’t new. What changed was that I started treating the years as something I was spending rather than something that was happening to me.

The same instinct runs outside of work. Rigor and wanderlust aren’t opposites. They’re one trait pointed at different problems, and both are ways of finding out what’s actually there instead of what you expected.
